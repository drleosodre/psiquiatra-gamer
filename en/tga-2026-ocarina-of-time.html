Ocarina of Time: the remake isn't up against the game, it's up against the memory

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

The Game Awards 2026 on the couch · text 2 of 8
A special series on the blog: one piece on the nominations controversy and one on each of the six Game of the Year nominees, up to the ceremony on December 10. Just arrived? Start with text 1, which opened the series.

On November 5, 2026, Nintendo released the remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, exclusively on Switch 2, rebuilt from scratch by the in-house team behind the series, with support from Monolith Soft. Twelve days later it was among the six Game of the Year nominees at The Game Awards. It debuted at 96 on Metacritic, the highest average of 2026 as of early November. The 1998 original is still the only game with a 99 on the site. And the usual fight is back: should a remake be in the running? Forbes went as far as asking how a remake of the highest-rated game in history fits an award like this.

The fight over the rules hides another question

Arguing over whether a remake can compete is arguing over the rules, and in practice the awards have already answered by nominating Final Fantasy VII Remake, Resident Evil 4 and now this one. The interesting question is a different one: what are we rewarding when we reward a game that so many people played as children? Those who defend the remake and those who attack it tend to talk as if there were two objects on the table, the 1998 game and the 2026 game. There are three. The third is the memory each person keeps of the first one, and it is that memory, more than the cartridge, that the remake is competing with.

Freud and the overdone yellow

In 1899 Freud published a short essay called Screen Memories (Über Deckerinnerungen). At its center is a scene told by a 38-year-old man: a sloping meadow full of dandelions, he and two young cousins picking flowers, the boys snatching the little girl's bunch, and then a piece of black bread that a farmer's wife cuts with a long knife. The scene seems trivial. What puzzles the narrator himself is its intensity: the yellow of the flowers, he says, stands out far too much from the whole, and the taste of the bread seems exaggerated in an almost hallucinatory way.

The analysis shows that the scene was used, years later, to give shape to fantasies from his youth, and that the yellow and the bread came from that later period. Freud stops short of calling the scene false. He says something more unsettling: "for the reports of our memory there is no guarantee at all" (the translations here are free renderings from the German). The scene may have happened, but it was picked out from many others because it served to represent something else, and along the way it was "chiselled", as he puts it.

At the end of the essay he generalizes. It may be doubtful whether we have memories from childhood at all, rather than merely memories about childhood. They show us our first years "not as they were, but as they appeared at later periods of awakening". And in those periods the memories did not emerge, as people usually say: "they were formed then".

The Hyrule nobody saw

Now swap the meadow for Hyrule. The 1998 Ocarina had a polygonal look that, in Game Informer's words, was shaped by the Nintendo 64's constraints, and it ran at around 20 frames per second that were not always stable, as Creative Bloq's review points out. Few people keep that image. What people keep is the overdone yellow: the huge field, the music, the fear of certain enemies, the sense of a world without edges. The memory was chiselled by everything that came after, including 28 years of people repeating that this was the greatest game of all time.

The remake seems to know this. It does not reconstruct the 1998 game: it swaps the visuals for something close to stylized realism and adds a free camera, a jump button, voiced cutscenes and even the option of humming the ocarina songs into the console's microphone. In an interview with Game Informer, producer Eiji Aonuma said the team tried to keep in place all the different "notes" people felt in the original. Not what was on the cartridge, but what was felt. And the critics judge it in the same register: Creative Bloq praised the remake for rekindling the magic of what it "must have been like" to play Ocarina for the first time, 28 years ago. Even the review measures the game against an imagined first time.

A game about waking up seven years later

There is a coincidence here that needs no forcing. Ocarina's premise is a coming of age: Link starts out as a child and, at a certain point, wakes up seven years later as an adult, in a world that changed while he was away. Whoever played it in 1998 is now making the same journey, with four times as much time in between: waking up as an adult in a redesigned Hyrule and sensing that something is not quite as it should be. Freud called the moments when childhood memories are formed periods of awakening. The remake is one of them. It does not give childhood back; it forces the memory to show itself as a memory.

A behind-the-scenes detail reinforces this. Aonuma said, in Nintendo's Ask the Developer series, that many members of the current team had only ever worked on the recent Zelda games: some knew the game vaguely, others had played it in their childhood. Part of the team that remade Ocarina did not carry the memory the public is defending.

What an award can actually reward

None of this says whether the remake deserves the award, and this is no place to predict the result. It says that the jury and the public will judge a 2026 game with an invisible competitor in the room. I have written here about retro-game nostalgia, the old game as a comforting object, and about the immersion adults lose. The point now is a different, rougher one. It is not only that memory softens the past. Memory is formed afterwards, for motives that have no commitment to historical accuracy, and it defends that product as if it were the original.

The question that remains applies far from the Switch 2. What from your childhood do you defend as if it were untouchable: a song, a house, a team, a person? And how much of it is the thing itself, and how much is the memory you formed of it later, at a time that had its own reasons for needing it to be that way?
