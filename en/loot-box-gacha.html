Loot boxes and gacha: why the box hooks you more than the prize

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~4 min read

Anyone who has opened a batch of boxes knows the sequence: the anticipation, the glow of the animation, the common item, the small letdown, and the hand reaching for the next box before the thought has even finished. Described from outside it looks irrational. Described from inside it is nearly automatic. And it is not an accident of design, it is the design.

The industry invented nothing, it copied

The mechanism has a name and a date. In 1957, Ferster and Skinner published a systematic study of reinforcement schedules, comparing what happens when reward arrives after a set number of actions, at fixed intervals, or unpredictably. The most cited result ever since concerns the variable-ratio schedule: reward after an unpredictable number of responses. It produces the highest response rates and, above all, is the most resistant to extinction, meaning the behaviour persists long after the reward stops coming.

Skinner used gambling as the example of this category, and it is exactly the format of the slot machine. Loot boxes and gacha do not resemble a casino by coincidence. It is the same schedule with better art.

Why the prize disappoints even when it is good

Here psychoanalysis adds what the behavioural curve does not explain: what happens after winning. Anyone who has pulled the rare item knows the strange sense that the best moment was the one before, and that the satisfaction lasts far less than the hunt did.

Freud offers a key in Instincts and their Vicissitudes, from 1915, where he breaks the drive down into source, pressure, aim and object. The decisive point is what he says about the object: it is the most variable element of the drive, is not originally connected with it, and is assigned to it only because it is peculiarly fitted to make satisfaction possible. In other words, the object is interchangeable. What persists is the circuit.

That changes how we read box addiction. The person is not after that specific character. The character is the pretext the circuit found this time. Which is why, once the item is obtained, desire does not close: it shifts to the next banner. A system that delivered genuine satisfaction would be terrible business, because it would end the circuit.

The lack has to be maintained, not resolved

Hence the architecture of these systems making so much commercial sense. There is always an item still to come. The collection never closes. The banner has a deadline. In-game currency comes in bundles that never quite match the price of what you want. None of this is carelessness. These are ways of keeping the lack in operation, because the lack is what the system lives on.

What this means in practice

Two conclusions, neither of them moralising. First: if you have felt unable to stop, that is not a character flaw. It is the expected result of a reinforcement schedule that was designed, studied and demonstrated to be the hardest to extinguish. Knowing this tends to relieve more than any promise of willpower.

The second is more practical: since the object is replaceable and the trigger is unpredictability, what works best is not resisting at the moment of the box, it is acting earlier, while the decision is still cold. Setting an amount before opening the game, removing the saved card, deciding outside the moment of excitement. Not because you are weak, but because in the instant of the animation you are playing against a mechanism that was measured in a laboratory and built to win.

And if the spending is already interfering with bills, sleep or relationships, it has stopped being a question of game design and become a question of life. It is worth talking to someone you trust, and worth seeking professional help if the weight is heavy.
