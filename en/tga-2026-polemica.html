GTA 6, Zelda and no Sony: why The Game Awards turned into a fan war

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

The Game Awards 2026 on the couch · text 1 of 8
Today the blog starts a special series. Over the next few days, up to the ceremony on December 10, there will be one piece on each of the six Game of the Year nominees, and a last one after the result. This first one is about the fight the nominations started.

On November 17 the six Game of the Year nominees for The Game Awards 2026 were announced, and within a few hours the comments had decided the awards got it wrong. Not for one reason but for three, each defended with an anger that none of the games, on its own, seems to justify.

The three fronts of the fight

The first is Grand Theft Auto VI. It leads with eight nominations and was nominated two days before it came out: it arrived on PS5 and Xbox Series on November 19, within the deadline set by the rules (November 20). Paul Tassi, at Forbes, pointed out that the nominations came a day and a half before the game was playable and asked whether enough jurors had played it in time. In the comments, the question turned into an accusation: "bought", "decided in advance".

The second is The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, the Switch 2 remake released on November 5. Tassi again questioned how a remake of the game with the highest average in Metacritic history fits an award like this. For many people, a remake should not compete with a new game.

The third is an absence. For the first time since 2014, no game published by Sony is among the Game of the Year nominees, as Push Square noted. Marvel's Wolverine got a lukewarm critical reception (the case already came up here in Marvel's Wolverine: the score arrived before the game, and so did the disappointment). Some read the absence as contempt.

I will not argue about who deserves it. The question here is a different one: why does a list of six names, chosen by a jury of media outlets, stir up so much in people who did not vote, gain nothing from the result and often have not even played the nominees?

Freud and the neighbors who hate each other

In Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), chapter V, Freud recalls that he had once dealt with the fact that neighboring communities, alike in almost everything, keep feuding with and mocking one another: Spaniards and Portuguese, North and South Germans, English and Scots. He called it the "narcissism of small differences" and admitted that the name does not explain much. But he added the essential point: this hostility offers a convenient and relatively harmless satisfaction of aggressiveness, one that makes it easier for the members of the group to hold together. And just before: it is always possible to bind a large number of people together in love, as long as there are others left over to receive the aggression.

Platform fandom is a textbook case. People who play on PlayStation and people who play on Switch have far more in common with each other than with people who do not play at all, and it is precisely this closeness that turns a small difference into a big offense.

Why the detail offends

The finer description is in an earlier book, Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921), chapter VI. There Freud sees, in open dislikes toward close strangers, a narcissism that seeks to assert itself and behaves as if any deviation from its way of being were a criticism of it and a demand to change. And he confesses he does not know why so much sensitivity should fasten on precisely these details.

If my game was left out of the TGA and my neighbor's got in, his nomination sounds like a verdict on me: on my taste, the hours I put in, the console I chose. The nominated remake offends those who wanted to see a new game there; the game nominated before release offends those who think merit is proven by playing. The argument may be good, and sometimes it is. The heat does not come from it.

In the same chapter, Freud points to the other side: inside the group, this intolerance disappears. The anger poured outward is the reverse side of the peace within.

When the brand takes the place of the ideal

Why does choosing a console become an identity? Here chapter VIII of the same book helps. (In the piece on the "GTA flu" I used chapter XI; the point now is a different one.) Freud describes the state of being in love and sums it up in a formula: "the object has been put in the place of the ego ideal". When that happens, criticism falls silent, and everything the object does seems right. Shortly after, he defines the group as people who have put one and the same object in the place of their ego ideal and, as a result, identified with one another.

Freud was thinking of leaders, churches and armies. But in chapter VI he was already considering that the leader might be replaced by an idea, an abstraction, and even that a shared hatred of a person or institution might unite as much as attachment does. A console brand is an abstraction of that kind, only with a logo. Whoever has put it in the place of the ideal starts measuring their own worth by the worth the world gives it, and an award becomes a report card on the whole group.

"Bought": the word that protects the ideal

The word "bought" spares one the work of admitting that a jury may simply have preferred something else. If the result is fraud, the ideal stays intact: we were not passed over, we were robbed. The fury says less about GTA 6, Ocarina or Wolverine than about who feels disregarded when their own does not appear on the list.

None of this makes the criticism false: nominating a game almost nobody had played is debatable, and the place of remakes deserves discussion. Psychoanalysis does not discredit arguments; it asks where the intensity with which they are defended comes from. And Freud called this hostility relatively harmless: a comment war over a video game award is one of the cheapest ways a group has of staying together.

Outside the game

The question holds far away from video games. When your team, your company, your profession or your circle is left out of some recognition, what exactly do you go out defending as if it were you, and how much of the offense is yours and how much is the group's?
