Why do we need to platinum? The psychoanalysis of 100%

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~6 min read

You finished the game. The story's over, the credits rolled, it was great. And then it hits you: you're missing the platinum trophy. Three collectibles short, one annoying challenge, one more difficulty setting. And, with no real desire to, you go back, hours hunting items on a map that no longer entertains you, just to see that 100%. Why isn't finishing enough? Why do we need the complete? The answer says less about the game and more about a voice we carry inside.

The voice that demands: the superego

Freud described a psychic agency that works like an inner judge: the superego. It's the part that demands, evaluates, and is never fully satisfied. Except the superego has a curious feature Freud noted: the more we give in to it, the more it demands. It doesn't deal in "good enough." It deals in "could be more." The platinum is a perfect target for that judge: a round number, objective, public, that turns "I enjoyed the game" into "I completed 100% of the task." And not completing it brings that specific discomfort, it isn't sadness, it's a reproach.

The ego-ideal: the image of who I ought to be

There's also the ego-ideal: the image of who we'd like to be, the model of excellence we chase. For many people, that image includes being someone competent, disciplined, who "finishes what they start," who leaves nothing half-done. The platinum becomes concrete proof of that image, a stamp that says "I am complete, I can handle it, I don't quit." Chasing 100% is, deep down, chasing a confirmation of worth. That's why the absence of one trophy bothers you so far beyond the game itself: it pokes at the question "am I enough?"

The pleasure that goes beyond pleasure

Here a detail appears that Lacanian psychoanalysis illuminates well. Notice that the platinum grind often isn't even fun anymore. It's repetitive, tiring, sometimes irritating, and we keep going. That's not ordinary pleasure (which makes us stop once we're satisfied); it's what Lacan called jouissance: a satisfaction that overshoots pleasure and verges on displeasure, a push that insists even as it hurts. The platinum hunter knows that border well: the point where chasing the last trophy stopped being enjoyable and became compulsion. Closing the list brings relief, but it's the relief of silencing the reproach, not the joy of play.

When it's healthy and when it's a trap

None of this condemns anyone who platinums, quite the opposite. Seeking full mastery of a game can be a legitimate source of pride, focus, and the pleasure of doing something with care. It's a different story when the 100% stops being a choice and becomes an obligation: when you platinum games you didn't even like just to avoid leaving them "incomplete," when the trophy list dictates your free time, when not finishing brings anxiety instead of "that's okay." At that point the platinum has stopped serving you and you've started serving it, the superego has taken the remote.

Leaving it incomplete is an exercise too

Maybe the most interesting act, for anyone who recognizes themselves here, is precisely the opposite of what the game asks: dropping a game halfway, on purpose, once it has already given you what was good about it. Not out of laziness, but as a small exercise in saying "enough, that was plenty" to that inner voice that never thinks it is. Because the most valuable lesson a game can offer may not be found in any trophy, but in the discovery that your worth doesn't depend on completing the list.
