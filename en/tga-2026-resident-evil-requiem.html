Resident Evil Requiem and the heroine who is afraid

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

The Game Awards 2026 on the couch · text 3 of 8
A special series on the blog: one piece on the nominations controversy and one on each of the six Game of the Year nominees, up to the ceremony on December 10. Just arrived? Start with text 1 or read the previous one, text 2.

Grace Ashcroft screams. RTÉ's review says she "shrieks and whimpers constantly" and that her anxiety is contagious: her panic becomes your own. GamesRadar+ sums it up: even with her book smarts, Grace "never feels powerful". Resident Evil Requiem made the Game of the Year shortlist betting on a protagonist like that. This is not a script detail. It is a thesis about what fiction does to us, and Freud put it into words in 1908.

The game, without spoilers

Requiem is the ninth game in Capcom's main series and came out on February 27, 2026 for PS5, Xbox Series X|S, Nintendo Switch 2 and PC. Grace is an FBI intelligence analyst, introduced in the official material through her skill at deduction and analysis. Her mother, Alyssa Ashcroft, survived the Raccoon City incident and kept investigating Umbrella as a reporter until her death; that death, the official text says, shook Grace "to the soul". A string of deaths linked to a mysterious disease takes Grace to the abandoned Wrenwood Hotel, the place where her mother was murdered.

At The Game Awards 2025, Capcom revealed the second protagonist: Leon S. Kennedy, another Raccoon City survivor, whose ruins the game revisits. The split was announced bluntly: with Grace, "terrifying survival horror"; with Leon, "pulse-pounding action". Both campaigns can be played in first or third person, but the game itself recommends Grace in first person, with the narrow field of view that turns any dark corner into a threat, and Leon in third. Critics liked it (89 on Metacritic for the PS5 version, 90 on OpenCritic), and Capcom announced more than 5 million units sold in less than a week.

His Majesty the Ego

In Creative Writers and Day-Dreaming (Der Dichter und das Phantasieren, 1908), Freud compares popular novels to daydreams and notices a shared trait: they all have a hero at the center, whom the author seems to protect "as if by a special providence". If one chapter ends with the hero unconscious and bleeding, the next finds him being cared for, on the road to recovery. We follow this hero with a feeling of safety that Freud sums up in a line by the playwright Ludwig Anzengruber: "Nothing can happen to you." And he concludes that in this revealing mark of invulnerability one easily recognizes "His Majesty the Ego, the hero of every daydream and every novel".

The blog has used this essay before, in the piece on jealousy of the video game, through another passage: the one saying that the opposite of play is not seriousness but reality. And the phrase should not be confused with the "His Majesty the Baby" of 1914, which appeared in the GTA analysis: there Freud is talking about the parents' narcissism revived in the child, who should be exempt from illness and death. Here the subject is the reader's ego, settled inside the hero.

Leon is the hero; Grace is the ego left over

Leon is the Majesty in its purest form: the legendary agent, the veteran who already made it out of Raccoon City alive and comes back to sort things out. With him, fantasy works the way Freud described: the player feels that nothing will happen, and enjoys the action precisely for that reason.

Grace is the reverse. No combat training, no one-liners, her head as her main resource. Survival horror is, in practice, the genre that strips the protagonist of that special providence: scarce supplies, every shot a last resort, a camera pressed to the face that cannot see what comes from behind. It is "nothing can happen to you" refuted as a game rule.

And Freud left room for this split. In the same essay, he traces the psychological novel to the modern writer's tendency to "split his ego, through self-observation, into partial egos" and to personify the conflicting currents of mental life in several heroes. Requiem does this with two protagonists. One carries the wish to be invulnerable; the other carries the knowledge that we are not. The game does not choose between them. It alternates.

Why we pay to be afraid

If the Majesty leaves the character, where does it go? To the couch. Freud notes that, because of the unreality of the fictional world, many excitations that are "in themselves distressing" can become a source of pleasure for those who listen and watch. Grace's fear is real in the player's body, but it comes surrounded by a frame that life does not provide. Invulnerability has not disappeared. It has changed address, from the hero to the player.

This is a different answer from the one the blog gave in why we like to feel afraid, which centered on actively mastering an anxiety suffered passively. The 1908 point is another one: the pleasure does not come from beating fear, it comes from being able to feel it without paying the price. Freud closes the essay suggesting that part of our enjoyment of the work comes from the writer allowing us to enjoy our own fantasies "without any reproach and without shame". What Requiem offers is something the classic hero rarely has: the right to be afraid without shame. To scream, back away, hide, and still be the protagonist.

An inheritance that is not a fighting move

Grace does not inherit a combat skill from her mother. She inherits a question, and her reporter mother's method for chasing it: investigating. Raccoon City, the destroyed city the series returns to, is the shared past of mother, daughter and Leon, but the game places grief on the side of the one who does not know how to fight. The invulnerable hero needs no inheritance; he arrives ready. The one who is afraid is the one who carries the story.

Off the screen, the split still stands. When you tell your own story, to others or to yourself, who is usually the protagonist: the one nothing touches, or the one who feels afraid and goes anyway? And which of the two would you rather no one saw?
