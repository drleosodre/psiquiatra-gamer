Toxic multiplayer: why we become someone else online

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~6 min read

It's almost a cliché, but it happens every day: a polite person, kind in real life, joins an online match and, fifteen minutes later, is cursing out a stranger's mother over a missed shot. They aren't "evil." Outside that lobby, they'd never do it. So what happens to us when the mic opens and the username hides the face? Psychoanalysis, together with the psychology of crowds, has an uncomfortable answer: toxicity isn't a rare deviation. It's what's left over when an invisible brake comes loose.

The superego: the brake we carry inside

In Freudian theory, what holds back our most aggressive impulses isn't only the police out there, it's an internal agency, the superego, the law we internalized as we grew up. It's what makes you feel shame, guilt, the "you don't do that" before any external punishment even arrives. The superego works, in large part, under the gaze of the other: we behave because we're seen, recognized, held accountable. Take away the face, take away the name, take away the consequences, and that brake starts to slip.

Anonymity and deindividuation

The username and the avatar do exactly that: they dissolve your recognizable identity. Psychology calls this phenomenon deindividuation, when a person stops feeling like a unique individual, responsible and observed, and starts acting as an anonymous part of a mass. With no face, no name, no track record, the moral bill seems never to come due. Subtract the shame (nobody knows who I am), subtract the fear of consequence (I'll never run into this person again), and what was repressed, the aggression, the contempt, the urge to humiliate, finds the door wide open. It's not that being online creates monsters; it's that it removes the brakes that kept the impulse locked away.

The crowd that dilutes guilt

Freud, in Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921), observed that in the group the individual does things they'd never do alone. Within the mass, responsibility is diluted: "everyone's doing it," "it wasn't just me." The lobby of a competitive game is a small mass of this kind, an environment where aggression becomes the norm, the insult becomes shared code, and each person feels less guilty because they're immersed in a collective mood. Toxicity feeds itself: the more people curse, the more cursing seems like "what you do around here."

Why defeat leaves us so raw

Then there's the emotional fuel. The competitive game stirs narcissism, victory inflates the ego, defeat wounds it. Losing, especially because of a mistake (yours or an ally's), reopens a wound of inadequacy, and the anger needs a destination. Alone, that anger would be swallowed; in the anonymous online space, it gets dumped on the teammate or the opponent. The other becomes a convenient container for a frustration that, deep down, is with one's own limits. Cursing your ally is easier than bearing the feeling of having failed.

Knowing this is already reclaiming the brake

None of this is an excuse, understanding the mechanism doesn't let anyone off the hook. But it serves another purpose: when you notice, in the heat of the match, that you're about to unload on a stranger, you can recognize what it is. It isn't justice, it's the anger at your own failure looking for a victim; it isn't you "being honest," it's the brake loosened by anonymity. Naming it gives back a little control. And maybe the most mature thing you can do in a toxic lobby is simple: to remember that, on the other side of the username, there's a person just as full of superego, frustration, and humanity as you.
