Cyberpunk 2077: when the other lives inside your head

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~4 min read

Cyberpunk 2077 (CD Projekt Red, 2020) has a premise too good to be merely science fiction. V carries in their skull a chip holding the recorded personality of Johnny Silverhand, a man dead for fifty years. The chip is not a silent passenger: it talks, disagrees, leans against the wall making remarks, and is gradually overwriting V brain. The game is the race against that process.

The double is not a clone, it is a competitor

Johnny is not a copy of V. The difference is what makes it interesting. He has another history, other political convictions, another way of solving problems, and a fairly explicit contempt for how V runs their life. He does not represent duplication, he represents an otherness installed in the most intimate place available. And the game is honest in showing that cohabitation is not neutral: V starts liking things they did not like, reacting in ways that were not theirs.

Rank, Freud and an old theme

The double is a theme psychoanalysis borrowed from literature and took seriously early on. In 1914 Otto Rank published a study devoted to it, linking the figure of the double to narcissism and to the fear of death. Freud drew on that work in 1919, in The Uncanny, quoting Rank at length and adding an observation that matters here: the double harks back to a time when the ego was not yet sharply differentiated from the external world and from other people.

That is the source of the particular unease the figure produces. The double is not frightening because it is monstrous. It frightens because it reminds us that the border between self and other is neither natural nor guaranteed: it was built, and what was built can give way.

Night City turns the body into inventory

The setting reinforces the argument instead of serving as backdrop. In Night City the body is modular: arm, eye, nervous system, all swappable for a better part. Cyberpsychosis, in the game universe, is what happens when someone implants beyond what they can integrate. It is a fictional idea, but it poses a legitimate question: how much of you can be replaced before the sum stops adding up?

And the game answers by shifting the question from the body to memory. V loses arms and survives. What actually threatens is someone thinking from the inside.

The endings are answers about who is left

That is why Cyberpunk endings work better than most RPG moral choices. None of them is a clean victory, and all of them answer the same question: who remains. There is the path where V hands over the body and Johnny goes on living in it. There is the one where V remains and Johnny is erased. There is the exit with the Nomads, preserving the bond at the cost of everything else. And there is the solitary ending, the hardest, where V goes alone and probably dies.

Notice there is no option of happy fusion, the two living peacefully in one body. The game refuses to offer that, and the refusal is consistent with what Rank and Freud described. The double is not a companion, it is a dispute over the same place.

The question that outlives the fiction

The chip is science fiction, but the situation is not so exotic. Everyone carries voices that are not theirs and that comment from within: the way a parent spoke to you, the demand of an old boss, the sentence of someone long gone who keeps appraising your choices. Psychoanalysis calls this identification, and it is not pathology, it is how the psyche is assembled.

What Cyberpunk 2077 does is push the process to its visible limit, with a progress bar and a deadline. And the question it leaves is reasonable for anyone who has never been near an implant: among the voices that decide for you, how many do you still recognise as your own, and how long has it been since anyone audited that?
