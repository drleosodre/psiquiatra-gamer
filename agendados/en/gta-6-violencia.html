Will GTA 6 make anyone violent? What Freud would say about the usual panic

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

Psychoanalytic countdown to GTA 6 · text 5 · 3 days to go
This is a special series on the blog, one piece a day up to the launch of GTA 6. Just arrived? Start with text 10 or read the previous one, text 6.

In September, when it came out that Miami-Dade County had been talking with Rockstar about using the airport, the transit system and other public spaces to promote GTA 6, the county's sheriff, Rosie Cordero-Stutz, pushed back. "Grand Theft Auto is a videogame franchise built around criminal activity," she told the Miami Herald. A few weeks later, her office clarified to PC Gamer that her comments were about the use of public assets only.

Back in January, a regional lawmaker in Russia had already called for the game to be banned or censored, arguing that allowing that kind of content amounts to corrupting the younger generations. And PC Gamer recalls that a former American attorney spent years campaigning against the series. The question comes back with every GTA: is this game going to make someone violent?

What the research supports, and what it doesn't

People who worry deserve a better answer than an eye roll. The American Psychological Association (APA) revisited the issue in 2020. According to the association, the task force behind its updated resolution found "a small, reliable association between violent video game use and aggressive outcomes, such as yelling and pushing," and "these research findings are difficult to extend to more violent outcomes."

The APA's press release opens bluntly: "There is insufficient scientific evidence to support a causal link between violent video games and violent behavior." The association's president at the time put it this way: "Attributing violence to video gaming is not scientifically sound and draws attention away from other factors, such as a history of violence, which we know from the research is a major predictor of future violence."

In other words, there is a small effect on mild aggression, and there is no basis for saying the game turns anyone into a criminal. So why does the panic return with every release, just as loud?

Freud in the audience

Around 1905 or 1906, Freud wrote a short essay on theater, "Psychopathic Characters on the Stage," which he never published. It only appeared in 1942, first in English, in The Psychoanalytic Quarterly.

His starting point is the ordinary spectator. He feels that nothing grand ever happens to him, and he long ago had to tone down his ambition to stand at the center of events. He wants to feel, to act, to shape the world as he pleases: he wants to be a hero. The playwright and the actors make that possible by letting him identify with one.

But the spectator knows that real heroism doesn't come without pain, suffering and grave fears, and that he only has one life. Hence the essay's central sentence, in James Strachey's translation: "his enjoyment is based on an illusion; that is to say, his suffering is mitigated by the certainty that, firstly, it is someone other than himself who is acting and suffering on the stage, and, secondly, that after all it is only a game, which can threaten no damage to his personal security." In the German, doch nur ein Spiel.

An illusion everyone agrees to

Illusion, here, doesn't mean being fooled. The spectator doesn't think the actor really died. He knows, and it's because he knows that he can let himself be carried away. It's an illusion agreed to by everyone in the room at once.

GTA bends that contract at one point and keeps it at another. The first certainty wears thinner, because it is no longer an actor doing the acting: you're the one pressing the button. Jason and Lucia are still other people, but they act through your hand. The second certainty, that it's only a game, stays fully intact.

My reading is that moral panic is born right there. It doesn't doubt that the game is a game. It doubts that the other person, the player, can hold up their end of the contract. We almost never fear for ourselves: we fear for the neighbor's teenager, for the next generation, for some faceless someone who might not be able to tell the difference. And so far, research hasn't found the bridge from controller to crime that this fear imagines.

The goat and the scapegoat

An almost hidden sentence in the essay helps here: "The fact that drama originated out of sacrificial rites (cf. the goat and the scapegoat) in the cult of the gods cannot be unrelated to this meaning of drama." Drama, Freud goes on, "appeases, as it were, a rising rebellion against the divine regulation of the universe, which is responsible for the existence of suffering."

The scapegoat carries everyone's guilt and is driven away. The stage inherited that function in civilized form: it puts someone else in our place to suffer. The panic around GTA, as I read it, makes the same move by another route. Real violence hurts, is diffuse and has many causes; the APA itself calls it a complex social problem. A single culprit, with a cover, a price and a release date, is far easier to think about. It can be pointed at, banned, driven out of town.

That is why we need a culprit so badly: it gives an address to an anxiety that had none. And here's the irony. The object chosen is precisely a game, a machine for putting someone else where the suffering happens. The panic does to the game what the game does to the player.

Worrying without needing a villain

None of this makes the concern silly. GTA is not a game for children, and deciding what your kid plays, and at what age, is care, not panic. The problem starts when worry becomes a shortcut, and pointing at the game spares us from looking at what research takes seriously, like a history of violence, which doesn't come in a box.

Freud wrote about an audience that knew it was at the theater. On November 19, millions of people will enter Vice City knowing it's Vice City. The question reaches well beyond the game: when something scares you, how long does it take for a culprit to show up, and what does that culprit spare you from thinking about?
