Spoilers: why some people chase the ending of GTA 6

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

Psychoanalytic countdown to GTA 6 · text 2 · launch day
This is a special series on the blog, one piece a day up to the launch of GTA 6. Just arrived? Start with text 10 or read the previous one, text 3.

Today, November 19, GTA 6 arrives on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S. Over the next few days, two scenes will play out again and again. In one, someone mutes the words "ending" and "GTA" on their phone and quietly leaves the office group chat. In the other, someone plays for a few hours, hits pause and goes looking up how it all ends.

It's an old fight, and Rockstar picked a side a long time ago. But before deciding who's right, it's worth asking what each of them is doing with the unexpected. Freud wrote a book in 1926 that helps here.

The rule that arrived before the game

On Rockstar's support site, a policy on what fans may post, last updated on January 10, 2025, asks that nothing "intentionally spoil the plot for others" and lists what can be taken down: video showing the ending of the game, edited combinations of cutscenes or plot dialogue, and any other big reveal in the story. Cutscenes are fine as part of a larger playthrough or a narrated Let's Play; isolated cinematics are not. If you're not sure whether something counts as a spoiler, there's even an email address you can write to and ask.

As the Brazilian site Critical Hits pointed out on November 4, the text never says when a spoiler stops being a spoiler, and takedowns can happen even after launch. Nor is the rule new. On September 17, 2013, the day GTA V came out, Kotaku reprinted almost the same clause and called it commendable, and also a little drastic: "if you don't want GTAV spoiled, don't go clicking play on that 'GTAV ENDING CINEMATICS [HD]' clip." Both sides of the fight already fit in a single news story.

The ones who chase the ending

Spoiler hunters are often told they're ruining their own fun. The research, on a small scale, doesn't back that up. In 2011, Jonathan Leavitt and Nicholas Christenfeld, of the University of California, San Diego, gave volunteers twelve short stories of three kinds (ironic twist, mystery and literary), some with the outcome revealed up front and some without. Across all three kinds, people preferred the spoiled versions.

These were short stories, not a game that runs dozens of hours, and an average doesn't settle anyone's taste. But spoiler hunters aren't sabotaging themselves. They're doing something else.

Freud and the alarm that goes off early

In Inhibitions, Symptoms and Anxiety, Freud revises his own theory and lands on a blunt sentence: anxiety is the reaction to danger. At the core of danger, he explains in an addendum at the end of the book, lies the estimate of our strength compared with the size of the threat, and the admission of our helplessness in the face of it. A situation of helplessness actually lived through, he calls traumatic.

Then comes the decisive step. It is an important advance in self-protection, Freud writes, "when this traumatic situation of helplessness is not merely awaited but is foreseen, anticipated." That is where the signal of anxiety is given: "I will act as if it were already present as long as there is still time to avert it." In the words of the 1936 authorized translation, "Anxiety, therefore, is the expectation of the trauma on the one hand, and on the other, an attenuated repetition of it."

And there's more: the ego, which went through the trauma passively, now actively repeats a weakened version of it, hoping to take the direction of its course into its own hands. In the same passage, Freud notes that children do just this in play, moving from passivity to activity.

Trading surprise for control

Now my own reading, on a much smaller scale: nobody is traumatized by a video game plot twist, but the surprise in a story is a small, made-to-order helplessness. For a moment, the story knows something you don't and decides when to tell you.

For the spoiler hunter, that moment is the danger situation. Reading the ending in a plot summary at 2 a.m. is giving yourself the attenuated dose: the shock still happens, but at a time of your choosing, at a size of your choosing, with no audience. When the scene finally comes up in the game, it no longer catches anyone off guard. What you lose in surprise you gain in command, and the 2011 study suggests plenty of people come out ahead on that trade.

Protecting the surprise

People who run from spoilers aren't immune to anxiety. They hear the alarm too; the danger, for them, is just a different one: what's under threat is the shock itself. Muting words and leaving the group chat are the protective actions the signal sets off. Freud observes that in the face of a real danger we develop two reactions, the affective one, an outbreak of anxiety, and a protective action, and that when things work well, one gives the signal for the other.

What the spoiler dodger protects is precisely the moment of not being in charge. They're betting they can handle being caught off guard by the story, and that this small helplessness, lived out on the couch, is part of the pleasure. For them, in that moment, surprise isn't a threat, it's a gift, and Rockstar's policy is on their side.

Neither one is wrong. Both are using the same apparatus Freud described, a signal that goes off early so we don't get caught off guard. What changes is what each thinks needs avoiding: the shock, or the lack of it. One wants to see how big the wave is before wading in; the other likes being carried off by it.

And it tends to show up far from video games: in the last page of a novel read first, in the movie synopsis checked before the theater, in the surprise party one person loves and another dreads. When life is about to tell you something you don't know yet, do you rush to read the ending, or do you let the scene come to you?
