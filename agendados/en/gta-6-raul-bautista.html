Raul Bautista on the couch: the robber who always raises the stakes

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

Psychoanalytic countdown to GTA 6 · text 9 · 7 days to go
This is a special series on the blog, one piece a day up to the launch of GTA 6. Just arrived? Start with text 10, which opened the countdown.

Nearly every GTA 6 character got a short introduction from Rockstar on the official site. Raul Bautista's has a quirk: to describe a bank robber, it borrows the vocabulary of somewhere else. The last line warns that, sooner or later, his crew "will have to double down or pull their chips from the table."

Chips, table, doubling down. That is not the language of the vault. It is the language of the casino, and I suspect the choice is not an accident.

What Rockstar has told us (and what it hasn't)

The official text is brief. It opens with confidence, charm, and cunning. Raul is "a seasoned bank robber always on the hunt for talent ready to take the risks that bring the biggest rewards." And the middle sentence is the one that matters here: "Raul's recklessness raises the stakes with every score."

That's all there is. No childhood, no trauma, no backstory, and I am not going to make one up. The game arrives on November 19, and what Raul does with that recklessness can only be found out by playing. What we can read right now is the shape of the sentence. It is not about one big score. It is about a score that is always bigger than the last one. The weight falls on the movement, not on the size.

Freud at the roulette table

In 1928, Freud wrote an essay to open a German volume of the drafts and sources of The Brothers Karamazov. It is called "Dostoevsky and Parricide," and most of it deals with the writer's relationship to his father and to guilt. But one passage is about something else: the period when Dostoevsky, living in Germany, was in the grip of roulette, an episode that his posthumous papers and his wife's diaries had just brought to light.

Dostoevsky claimed he gambled to win enough to go back to Russia without being jailed by his creditors. That was a pretext, and the writer himself, Freud notes, was sharp enough to see it and honest enough to admit it. What mattered was "gambling for its own sake," le jeu pour le jeu.

Playing to lose

Then comes the observation that gives this section its title. "He never rested until he had lost everything. For him gambling was another method of self-punishment." Time after time he promised his young wife he would stop, and he almost always broke his word. After the ruin came a second satisfaction: scolding and humiliating himself in front of her, inviting her to despise him. Once his conscience was unburdened, the cycle started over the next day.

There is a detail that is fully visible only in German. In the English version, "Dostoevsky's burden of guilt had taken a tangible shape as a burden of debt." In German, guilt is Schuld and debts are Schulden. The debt gives guilt a body. It turns something diffuse into a bill that can finally be collected. And there is a curious fact his wife had noticed: his writing never went better than after they had lost everything and pawned their last possessions. Once the penalty was paid, Freud comments, the inhibitions on his work eased.

Freud goes further and suggests that this compulsion repeated an older one from childhood. It is the part of the essay most likely to raise a modern reader's eyebrow. You don't have to buy the whole hypothesis to keep what is useful in it: the object of a compulsion can change while its form stays the same. The irresistible temptation, the solemn vow of never again, the numbing pleasure and the bad conscience afterward survive the substitution intact, he says.

Why the score has to be bigger

Now go back to Rockstar's sentence. If all Raul wanted were the money, one clean job would do, and he would stop. Recklessness that raises the stakes every time says something else. Read with Freud's yardstick, and this is a reading by analogy, since Raul is a one-paragraph character, the escalation may be a way of making sure that, sooner or later, the bill comes due. Whoever keeps raising the stakes is not just chasing the prize. They are also calling for the day they lose.

That does not make Raul a clinical case, and I have no intention of treating him as one. It makes him a good dramatic question. The official description already hands the problem to whoever is around him: the crew will have to decide whether to ride the climb or get up from the table.

A genre that can't stop, and the people who play

Heist movies tend to run on the same engine. It is almost a convention that there is a "last job" that turns out not to be the last, or that turns out to be the one that goes wrong. Audiences accept it without complaint, because they know the story needs a bigger risk in every act.

Players know a far more innocent version of this. You finish the campaign and crank up the difficulty. Most of the time it is simply the pleasure of mastering something hard, and there is nothing unhealthy about it. But it is worth noticing when the bar keeps rising on its own and no win ever feels like enough. Sometimes the question is not how much the prize is worth, but what we are trying to pay off.

Raul is still a face and a paragraph, and his story will only be told in November. The question he leaves, though, works outside Leonida: in your own life, which bet do you keep raising, and what are you really after when you do it, the prize or the moment you finally have to pay?
