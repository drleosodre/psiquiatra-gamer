Control Resonant: the fear no one received comes back nameless

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

The Game Awards 2026 on the couch · text 5 of 8
A special series on the blog: one piece on the nominations controversy and one on each of the six Game of the Year nominees, up to the ceremony on December 10. Just arrived? Start with text 1 or read the previous one, text 4.

Control Resonant (Remedy, 2026) begins with a man who spent years confined and studied and is now let loose in a city that has stopped obeying physics. Dylan Faden, brother of Jesse, the protagonist of the first Control, is sent into the field by his own captors, the Federal Bureau of Control, at the height of a supernatural crisis: a cosmic force has invaded Manhattan, distorts gravity and reorders reality into what Remedy calls a "geophysical nightmare". The official announcement says Dylan struggles to comprehend and contain the dangers while he searches for his sister. Comprehend and contain. It is hard to write, by accident, a sentence closer to Wilfred Bion.

From a building that moves to a city that moves

For those who never played the first one: Control (2019) took place in the Oldest House, headquarters of an American government agency that investigates the unexplainable. The building changes shape as you walk through it, walls fold away like paper, and the Bureau catalogues ordinary objects that have acquired a strange force: a rubber duck, a traffic light, a flamingo. It was bureaucracy trying to file what fits in no folder at all.

Resonant came out on September 24 for PS5, Xbox Series X|S and PC, takes place seven years later and carries the problem outside: now it is the city that will not keep still. It became an action RPG and swapped the pistol for a melee weapon that changes shape; Remedy promises you do not need to know the previous game. Critics liked it more than the original (84 on Metacritic, against 82), but not unanimously: GameSpot gave it a 9, Eurogamer a 3 out of 5.

Thoughts in search of someone to think them

In "A Theory of Thinking" (1962), Bion turns common sense upside down. We imagine that first there is someone who thinks and then come the thoughts. Bion proposes the opposite: first come the thoughts, which require an apparatus to cope with them; only afterwards does that apparatus develop, the one he provisionally calls thinking. And he insists: thinking has to be called into existence to cope with thoughts.

A thought, here, is raw experience that arrives before there is anyone to receive it: hunger, fear, the feeling of coming apart. The Control universe works like that. The forces arrive first. The Bureau, with its forms, is the belated attempt to assemble an apparatus able to cope with them. And the warped Manhattan of Resonant is the image of a world where things have arrived and no one has managed to think them yet.

The fear that comes back without a name

The strongest point lies in the origin of that apparatus. Bion describes the infant who feels, say, that it is dying, and on its own has nothing it can do with that. Normal development depends on its being able to project this feeling into the mother and to take it back after its sojourn in the breast has made it tolerable. Bion names the maternal capacity that allows this: reverie, an availability to take in what the infant cannot yet think.

And when it fails? The sentence is blunt: if the projection is not accepted by the mother, the infant feels that its feeling that it is dying is stripped of such meaning as it has. It therefore takes back in not a fear of dying made tolerable, but a nameless dread. The fear returns larger and without form.

Bion has appeared here before, on psychosis in Hellblade and on hype frustration in Marvel's Wolverine. Now the question is a different one: what happens to an experience when no one receives it.

Being studied is not being understood

Dylan embodies that failure. The announcement speaks of years of confinement at the hands of the Bureau; reviews speak of confinement and testing, of a lifetime of isolation and containment. Containment is the key word. In the Bureau's vocabulary, to contain is to lock up, measure, record. In Bion, receiving an experience is almost the opposite: it means letting it pass through someone and come back with meaning.

The 1962 paper has an expression that fits an institution like that: instead of an understanding object, the infant is left with a wilfully misunderstanding object, with which it is identified. Being observed for years is not being understood. It may be the most efficient way of not being understood.

Hence the precision of the premise. The Bureau, which locked him up instead of receiving him, now needs him and sends him into a city where the unthought has taken physical form. Reviews describe him as awkward with people, driven by guilt, useful to an institution that had already written him off. Remedy also promises a metaphysical space reflecting Dylan's troubled psyche; one review calls these zones pockets of broken reality. And the bosses, the Resonants, were people once, according to another review, and became something enormous and wrong. It is the portrait of experience that never found anyone to think it.

Same studio, different problem

Remedy has been stitching its games into a shared universe since 2020, when a Control expansion brought Alan Wake into the Bureau. The comparison is tempting, but the problem is a different one. In the piece on Alan Wake, the issue was the writer whose material escapes him: sublimation that fails, the double that returns wearing his face. Alan has an apparatus for thinking, and it overflows. Dylan is one step earlier. What he lacked was not mastery over his own creation, it was someone to receive what he felt. Resonant is not about taming the darkness; it is about building, late and under pressure, the apparatus that should have been built by two.

The question that stays outside the game

No one needs to have been a secret agency's test subject to know the difference between being understood and being recorded. It shows up in the conversation where you share a fear and get back a ready-made solution or a change of subject. The fear does not go away. It returns more diffuse.

So here is the question: when you bring someone something that has no name yet, does that person give back something you can think about, or do they just note it down and file it? And when someone brings that to you, which of the two do you usually do?
