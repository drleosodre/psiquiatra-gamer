The day after: what's left when the wait for GTA 6 is over

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

Psychoanalytic countdown to GTA 6 · text 1 · the day after
This is the last text in a special series on the blog, one piece a day up to the launch of GTA 6. Just arrived? Start with text 10 or read the previous one, text 2, from launch day.

This series counted down one text a day. Today, November 20, the counter has run out. GTA 6 came out yesterday on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S, more than thirteen years after GTA V, which launched on September 17, 2013.

Some people have already seen the credits, others have barely left the tutorial, and some have no plans to play at all. This piece holds whatever the verdict on the game turns out to be, because it's about something else that changed overnight: the wait is over.

When waiting becomes an occupation

Over thirteen years, waiting for GTA 6 became an activity with its own routine: memes about the delays, countdown accounts, fans comparing trailers frame by frame and tracking moon cycles for clues. In the piece on fort/da, I read that wait as a game for mastering an absence, and I ended by asking what happens to all of it once the thing we were waiting for finally arrives.

It loses its purpose, of course. But something that filled years and brought so many people together doesn't vanish without a trace. Freud wrote a short essay about a close relative of this experience: the ending that spoils the pleasure before it even arrives.

A summer walk

On Transience was written in November 1915 and published in 1916 in a commemorative volume put together by the Berlin Goethebund. Freud recounts a walk through a blooming summer landscape with a taciturn friend and a young but already famous poet, whom some identify as Rilke, though that has never been confirmed.

The poet admired the beauty around them but took no joy in it. He was troubled by the thought that all of it would be gone by winter, like all human beauty and everything beautiful that people have made or might make. Whatever he would otherwise have loved seemed robbed of its worth by its destiny of being transient.

Freud describes two possible reactions to that thought: the poet's aching weariness with the world, or a rebellion that insists so much beauty must somehow last. He rejects both. The demand for immortality, he writes, is too plainly a product of our wishes to lay claim to reality, and "what is painful may none the less be true."

Scarcity value in time

What Freud disputes is the idea that transience lessens the worth of what is beautiful. "On the contrary, an increase! Transience value is scarcity value in time." Limiting the chance to enjoy something makes the enjoyment more precious. A flower that blooms for a single night, he says, seems no less splendid for it.

Translating this to video games, on my own account: a game that will end someday, however big the map, has something of the one-night flower about it. The first time in each place only happens once. By Freud's measure, that takes nothing away from those first hours; it gives them weight.

Why the argument didn't land

Freud found his arguments incontestable and noticed they had made no impression on either the poet or the friend. He concluded that a powerful emotional factor was clouding their judgment: what spoiled their enjoyment of beauty must have been "a revolt in their minds against mourning." The thought that it would pass gave them both a foretaste of mourning, and since the mind instinctively recoils from what is painful, their pleasure was impaired.

Notice the reversal. The poet isn't suffering because summer is over: summer is right there, in bloom. He suffers in advance. As I read it, this is a sad bit of economics: you pay for future mourning with present pleasure.

The small mourning for the wait

In the same essay, Freud admits we don't know why detaching the libido from its objects should be so painful. What we can see is that it clings to them and won't give up the lost ones even when a substitute is ready at hand.

For anyone who waited, the wait was an object too, something you put feeling into, and its substitute, the game itself, has arrived. Even so, you might miss the countdown, the theories, the group chat that picked apart every trailer. It's a small mourning, a little embarrassing to admit, and it doesn't depend on whether the game is good or bad.

Nor is it the mourning I wrote about in another piece, for the idealized game that dies when the real one arrives. Here, even if the game outdoes every fantasy, what's lost is the wait itself. And Freud's poet illustrates a second risk: sliding from mourning the wait into mourning the game in advance. Going slow so it won't end, putting off the last mission, eyeing the credits as a threat. Whoever chases the ending, the subject of yesterday's piece, faces the end differently; whoever rations the game so it never ends may be walking through summer with their eyes on winter.

After winter

Freud ends the essay with the war, which broke out a year after that walk and robbed the world of its beauties. Many came to feel that what had proved so fragile had lost its worth; to him, they were simply in mourning. And mourning, however painful, runs its course on its own: once it has renounced everything lost, it leaves the libido free for other objects, just as precious, or more so.

All proportions kept, the wait for GTA 6 will pass too, and the feeling that was in it has somewhere to go: it's right there in the console, or in line for it. The question the poet leaves reaches well beyond Leonida: what in your life are you failing to enjoy because you're already mourning its end?

This series was transient too, and if Freud was right, that is what gave each of its texts its worth.
