Phantom Blade Zero: the hero who plays with 66 days to live

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

The Game Awards 2026 on the couch · text 6 of 8
A special series on the blog: one piece on the nominations controversy and one on each of the six Game of the Year nominees, up to the ceremony on December 10. Just arrived? Start with text 1 or read the previous one, text 5.

"What's your plan, knowing you have only 66 days to live?" That question opened the announcement of Phantom Blade Zero on the PlayStation Blog in 2023. Three years later, S-Game's title is one of the six Game of the Year nominees at The Game Awards 2026, and the question is still the most interesting thing about it, because it asks the player for something that, according to Freud, our minds cannot do.

Sixty-six days

In the official premise, Soul is an elite assassin serving a powerful organization known simply as The Order. Framed for the murder of The Order's patriarch and gravely injured in the manhunt, he is saved by a mystic healer, but the makeshift cure only lasts 66 days, the time he has to find out who set him up. The PlayStation Store sums it up: a swordsman with a wounded heart and only sixty-six days left to live.

The game came out on October 29, 2026, for PS5 and PC. It is a dark wuxia with fast combat, which Eurogamer compared to a high-octane Sekiro. It launched with a Metacritic average of 88: critics praised the weapons and bosses and complained about a fragmented story. It is also the first large-scale production from S-Game, a Chinese studio, and it reaches the top category two years after Black Myth: Wukong, another Chinese game, was nominated for it.

No one believes in their own death

In 1915, in the middle of the First World War, Freud published Thoughts for the Times on War and Death. The second part, "Our attitude towards death", opens with an accusation: that attitude was never sincere. Out loud, death is natural and inevitable; in practice, we act as if it were not, trying to eliminate it from life.

The reason comes next. Our own death is unimaginable, and whenever we try to imagine it we notice that we are still there, as spectators. Hence the statement that the psychoanalytic school, in his words, dared to make: at bottom no one believes in his own death, or, what amounts to the same thing, in the unconscious every one of us is convinced of his own immortality (im Unbewußten sei jeder von uns von seiner Unsterblichkeit überzeugt).

Here is the first irony of Phantom Blade Zero. The game writes Soul's deadline into the premise, but whoever holds the controller remains a spectator of the scene. We know Soul's days are numbered; we do not believe it. Freud suggests this may even be the secret of heroism: many heroes face danger not out of calculation but out of the certainty of a character by the playwright Anzengruber, whom he quotes in Viennese dialect: Es kann dir nix g'scheh'n, nothing can happen to you. It is the same line that came up in text 3 of the series, on Resident Evil Requiem, through another essay by Freud; here it takes on the weight of death.

The life that grows poor

Leaving death out, Freud says, has a price. Life becomes impoverished and loses its interest when the highest stake in the games of life (der höchste Einsatz in den Lebensspielen), life itself, must not be risked. It turns as hollow as a flirtation in which everyone knows from the start that nothing is going to happen. And the tendency to leave death out of the calculation of life (den Tod aus der Lebensrechnung auszuschließen) brings many other renunciations and exclusions in its wake.

The metaphor is a game metaphor. Freud is not asking us to think about death all the time: when it drops out of the sum, the whole sum comes out wrong, and we live as if we had infinite time.

In fiction, many lives

From this comes a conclusion that seems written for video games. Since real life has grown poor, we seek compensation in fiction, where there are still people who know how to die and where the condition for reconciling ourselves with death is met: having, behind all the vicissitudes, an untouchable life in reserve. Life is like chess, where one wrong move forces us to give up the game, except that there is no rematch. In fiction we find the plurality of lives we need: we die in identification with one hero, outlive him, and are ready to die again, unharmed, with another.

No fiction has fulfilled this as literally as games, as Dark Souls showed. Phantom Blade Zero takes the same path in combat: according to reviews, dying costs almost nothing, and defeated enemies do not come back. Death in combat has been disarmed, and the deadline stays in the story: according to GamingBolt, Soul's days tick down as the plot moves forward. It is the combination Freud describes, a doomed hero and a player with lives to spare.

The mode with no rematch

S-Game, however, added a curious exception. After a full campaign on certain difficulties, the 66 Days mode unlocks, and the days become chances: Soul can die 66 times, and on the last one the game ends and deletes the single save file, which allows no manual saves. It is Freud's chess, where one wrong move ends the game and there is no rematch.

The mode gives back to the game the stake that, for Freud, civilized life lacked. Every mistake starts to weigh, and it is no accident that GamingBolt argued this is how the game should be played.

To bear life

That same year, Freud wrote On Transience, which I read in the piece on the day after GTA 6. There the question was the end of the things we love; here it is our own end, which the unconscious refuses to register. Freud closes the war essay by rewriting an old saying. Instead of Si vis pacem, para bellum (if you want peace, prepare for war), he proposes Si vis vitam, para mortem: if you want to bear life, prepare for death. Giving death the place it deserves, he writes, would make life more bearable, and bearing life remains the first duty of all who live.

Soul knows how many days he has. We do not, and the unconscious is grateful. But the question remains, and it holds far away from any console: if your deadline were written somewhere you saw it every day, what would you stop putting off?
