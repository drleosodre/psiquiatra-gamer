GTA 6, the favorite: why it's so easy to root against the giant

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

The Game Awards 2026 on the couch · text 7 of 8
A special series on the blog: one piece on the nominations controversy and one on each of the six Game of the Year nominees, up to the ceremony on December 10. Just arrived? Start with text 1 or read the previous one, text 6.

Grand Theft Auto VI was nominated for eight Game Awards before anyone could buy it. The list came out on November 17; the game reached stores two days later, on November 19, on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S. No other contender got as many nominations, and the reviews that followed turned an advantage into overwhelming favoritism. This piece is not about who will win on the 10th. It is about what a favorite this big stirs up in the people watching.

The favorite that arrived before it arrived

The eight nominations are Game of the Year, Best Game Direction, Best Narrative, Best Art Direction, Best Score and Music, Best Audio Design, Best Action/Adventure Game and Best Performance, the last one for Manni L. Perez, who plays Lucia Caminos. Nominating a game nobody had played was one of the points of the controversy in text 1, and it came after a wait so long it became a game in itself.

The reviews, released on November 18, changed the tone: IGN, GameSpot and Game Informer gave it a 10; Eurogamer and GamesRadar+, five stars. On November 20, the day I checked, the game had a 99 top critic average on OpenCritic and a 99 on Metacritic (PS5 version, according to the gaming press). That 99 ties Metacritic's all-time record, which until then belonged only to The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, from 1998, whose remake, ironically, is up against it for the main award. As of that date, Take-Two had not released official launch sales figures.

Rooting against the giant

A favorite like this is all it takes for many people to start, almost without noticing, rooting for someone else. Part of that is legitimate taste, and part is argument: some feel an award should recognize risk, not budget. But there is a remainder the arguments don't explain, a certain pleasure in picturing the envelope with another name inside.

That remainder shows up far from video games too: in the final where the small team faces the big one, in the coworker promoted too fast. When the giant wins, it gains nothing it didn't already have. When it loses, it becomes a story.

Those wrecked by success

In 1916, in the essay "Some Character-Types Met with in Psycho-Analytic Work", Freud devoted the second part to a figure that puzzled him: those wrecked by success (Die am Erfolge scheitern). The rule psychoanalysis had established was that people fall ill from frustration, when the satisfaction they desire is denied. So it was surprising to find people who fall ill precisely when a deep, long-cherished wish comes true. "It seems then as though they were not able to tolerate their happiness," he writes.

One of his examples is a professor who for years wished to succeed the master who had introduced him to science. Once chosen, he began to hesitate, played down his own merits and declared himself unworthy of the post. Freud explains it in one sentence: it is not at all unusual for the ego to tolerate a wish as harmless while it exists as fantasy and seems far from fulfillment, yet defend itself hard against it as soon as it approaches fulfillment and threatens to become reality. What blocks the gain, Freud says, are forces of conscience (Gewissensmächte), which forbid the person to reap from the happy change the gain they waited so long for.

A necessary caution: Freud is talking about falling ill, and no one here is falling ill or being diagnosed, least of all a studio. What interests me is the structure, which he himself preferred to examine in stage characters, Shakespeare's Lady Macbeth and Ibsen's Rebecca West, rather than in clinical cases.

When winning becomes an obligation

Take that sentence to the audience. While GTA 6 was a promise, the wish to see it huge was harmless: it fit into trailers, countdowns, memes. Now that it has arrived with a 99 and eight nominations, triumph is no longer fantasy and threatens to become a done deal. Then, in many people, the defense kicks in: digging out the lone 80 among the 10s, finding the record overblown, preferring the award go to someone else "so it isn't too obvious".

For the favorite, winning becomes an obligation, and an obligation fulfilled moves no one; losing becomes a scandal. It is a math in which full triumph has almost nowhere to be enjoyed, not even by those who rooted for it. This is not what I discussed in another piece, about the idealized game that disappoints when the real one arrives. There, reality falls short of the fantasy. Here, according to the critics, it didn't, and the discomfort comes precisely from that. What bothers us is not the flaw: it is the lack of one.

Whatever the envelope says

Freud closes the chapter by saying that these forces of conscience are closely connected with the Oedipus complex, with the relation to father and mother, as perhaps our sense of guilt in general is. I won't stretch the bridge further than it can bear. But it isn't hard to recognize, in rooting against the giant, an old scene: the small one facing the big one, wishing to see it fall and feeling guilty for wishing it.

That is why the result on the 10th works as a mirror, whatever it is. If GTA 6 wins, it is worth noticing whether the reaction is joy or a deflated "obviously". If it loses, it is worth noticing whether, along with the surprise, there comes a relief that is hard to justify. Either way, what shows up is our relationship with full success, other people's and our own.

And outside the game: where in your life is it more comfortable to see yourself as the underdog than to admit that, this time, you are the favorite? And when an old wish finally comes close to being fulfilled, who, in there, starts rooting against it?
