Why Animal Crossing calms you down: the game as a transitional object

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~4 min read

Animal Crossing has no boss, no game over, no hurry. You fish, plant, pay off an interest-free debt to a raccoon and chat with animals who ask how you are doing. Described that way it sounds like very little. And yet, in the year the whole world was shut indoors, this was the game millions of people opened every single day. It is worth asking what exactly it does.

What soothes is not the escape

The easy explanation is escapism: the island is cute, reality was awful, people fled. The trouble is that escapism does not explain the format. If it were only flight, the ideal would be total fantasy with no obligations. But Animal Crossing is full of obligations: there is debt, there are daily chores, there are plants that die if you disappear. It does not remove life demands, it offers a version of them at a bearable scale.

And there is the detail that defines everything: the game runs on the real clock. If it is night at your house, it is night on the island. It does not take you out of time, it keeps pace with your time. That is the opposite of flight.

Winnicott and the first possession that is not the baby

In 1953, in a paper with a precise subtitle, a study of the first not-me possession, Winnicott described something anyone who has lived with a small child knows: the blanket, the teddy, the grubby cloth that must not be washed. He called these objects transitional, and his finding was not noticing them, it was understanding the position they occupy.

The transitional object is neither the mother nor the baby. It sits in a third place, between the inner and the outer world, and Winnicott insisted that the child is not to be asked whether she created it or whether it was already there. The question must not be put. It is precisely this protected ambiguity that lets the object work as a bridge: it helps bear absence without requiring anyone to deny that absence exists.

The island as an intermediate area

Animal Crossing occupies exactly this position. The island is not the real world, and everyone knows it. But neither is it pure private fantasy, because it has its own rules, people turn up without being summoned, and there are consequences if you abandon it. It is an intermediate space, predictable enough to hold and unpredictable enough to stay interesting.

Winnicott used another term for the condition that makes this possible: holding. An environment that holds is not one that satisfies everything. It is one that is reliable enough for a person to lower their guard. The island does that: nothing terrible follows from a mistake, nobody leaves over a failure, and tomorrow it will still be there. At a time when reality was doing exactly the opposite, that reliability was not a small thing.

Playing is serious, and it is not the same as being distracted

There is a point in Winnicott that tends to get softened when he is quoted: for him, playing is not a pastime, it is an activity in which the subject is constituted. It is in playing that one experiments with being someone without reality immediate bill. In that sense, decorating a virtual house or spending forty minutes choosing where to plant a tree is not wasted time. It is the use of a space where choices matter little and can therefore be made freely.

The honest distinction is worth making, because not every game that fills time does this. There is what holds you and there is what merely fills the hours, and the practical test is simple: after closing it, do you feel slightly more whole, or just anaesthetised for another stretch? Animal Crossing, for a great many people, landed on the first side. Not because it solved anything, but because it offered a reliable corner in which to exist while nothing was solved.
