The $100 edition: what we buy in the "extra" of GTA 6

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

Psychoanalytic countdown to GTA 6 · text 6 · 4 days to go
This is a special series on the blog, one piece a day up to the launch of GTA 6. Just arrived? Start with text 10 or read the previous one, text 7.

In Rockstar's announcement of what comes with the GTA 6 Ultimate Edition, one line shows up five times: "Only open for business with the Ultimate Edition." It applies to a hair salon, a clothing store, a tattoo parlor with over 50 designs by the artist collective FAILE, and two car mod shops. Whoever sticks with the Standard will find those five doors closed.

The difference has an exact price. In the US, the Standard Edition costs $79.99 and the Ultimate $99.99. In Brazil, on the PlayStation Store and the Xbox store, it is R$449.90 versus R$549.90, and anyone who starts with the cheaper one can pay the R$100 upgrade later. The question is: what exactly do we buy with that "extra"?

Nine out of ten

Plenty of people have already answered with their wallets. In August, Strauss Zelnick, CEO of Take-Two, Rockstar's parent company, told CNBC that among people who have already bought the game, the scale tips toward the premium edition. He conceded himself that the earliest buyers may simply be the most avid consumers.

A few days later, Karl Kontus of the market intelligence firm Sensor Tower put the Ultimate's share of tracked purchases at 89%. He told GamesIndustry.biz that premium editions usually account for 10 to 20% of sales and that he had never seen a 90% premium share. It is an estimate, not an audited figure, and Kontus himself expected the share to drop over time. Still, the message is clear: for a lot of people, the expensive edition has become the normal one.

What's in the box

Rockstar describes the Ultimate as an exclusive collection of premium vehicles, weapons, apparel and action threaded across all aspects of Jason and Lucia's story, with new items uncovered behind each chapter. The store copy opens with an invitation to seize everything this massive world has to offer. The list includes a '95 Grotti Cheetah, engraved revolvers, abandoned classic cars to track down and restore, a raid on a gang compound, and those shops.

Brazilian outlet Canaltech reported players convinced that some of this content was cut from the base game to become a pricier edition, and noted that these are cosmetics that could have been available to everyone. What interests me is the word "everything" in the pitch. Most of the list is looks, vehicles, weapons and shops. What is on offer, before any single item, is never going without.

Freud and the palliatives

In 1930, in the second chapter of Civilization and Its Discontents, Freud starts from a blunt observation: life as we find it is too hard for us, with too much pain, too many disappointments, too many impossible tasks. "We cannot do without palliative remedies," he writes in Joan Riviere's authorized translation. He counts perhaps three: powerful diversions of interest, which lead us to care little about our misery; substitutive gratifications, which lessen it; and intoxicating substances, which make us insensitive to it. The substitute gratifications that art offers, he adds, are illusions in contrast to reality, but no less satisfying to the mind, thanks to the role fantasy plays in mental life.

Freud was talking about art; I put video games on the same shelf. And the chapter goes further. Under the pressure of so much possible suffering, he writes, people tend to scale down their claims to happiness: they count themselves happy simply for having escaped unhappiness, and the task of avoiding suffering pushes the task of obtaining pleasure into the background.

Paying not to see the closed door

Here, as I read it, is what that extra money buys. Not exactly more pleasure: a virtual hair salon is unlikely to change anyone's experience. It buys one less displeasure, that of walking past a closed door inside your own game. In Freud's terms, the Ultimate works less toward gaining pleasure and more toward the avoidance of unpleasure (Unlustvermeidung). It is insurance against the feeling of owning the incomplete version.

This is not the same as FOMO, which I have written about before. FOMO is wanting what others want, the fear of being left out of the party. Here the party is private: nobody needs to see your map for the closed door to bother you.

The "extra" never settles the bill

Freud has bad news for anyone who wants the complete package. Happiness in the strict sense, he argues, comes from the fairly sudden satisfaction of needs that have been dammed up, and by its very nature can only be a passing episode. Any situation we long for, once prolonged, yields only mild comfort: "we are so constituted that we can only intensely enjoy contrasts, much less intensely states in themselves." In a footnote he quotes Goethe, for whom nothing is harder to bear than a succession of fine days, and grants that this may be an exaggeration.

A map with every door open is not a map without lack. The lack just moves to a new address: the car that didn't come with it, the update that isn't out yet, the next edition. The "extra" soothes the wish to have everything for a while, but it never settles the bill, because what it promises is not an object, it is a state. And a state, according to Freud, is exactly what we enjoy least.

None of this says which edition to buy. In the same chapter, Freud warns: "There is no sovereign recipe in this matter which suits all; each one must find out for himself by which particular means he may achieve felicity." Whoever plans to spend a hundred hours in Leonida and wants that FAILE tattoo on Lucia's arm has their reasons. Whoever sticks with the Standard has theirs.

The question that remains reaches beyond the game: of everything you pay extra for in life, how much is for what you want to have, and how much is just so you never have to look at a closed door?
