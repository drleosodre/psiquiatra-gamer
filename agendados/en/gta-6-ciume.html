Is playing GTA 6 cheating? Jealousy of the video game

Leonardo Sodré · psychiatrist, psychoanalyst and gamer ~5 min read

Psychoanalytic countdown to GTA 6 · text 4 · 2 days to go
This is a special series on the blog, one piece a day up to the launch of GTA 6. Just arrived? Start with text 10 or read the previous one, text 5.

On August 27, a long gameplay showcase of GTA 6 went live, and the topic that traveled furthest afterward, according to Gulf News, was not a gun, a car or a heist. It was hand-holding. On TikTok, creator Shelby Haas summed up the objection in a line that spread fast: "Don't let your boyfriend or fiancé or husband play GTA 6." Her fiancé, Alex Eubank, posted his own response: "Do I want to play GTA VI? Of course I want to, but if it bothers my woman, I won't be playing." Other creators went further and called it infidelity, plain and simple.

On the other side, plenty of people pointed out that The Sims has let players build virtual families for a quarter of a century without any comparable panic. I'm not here to referee the fight, least of all anyone's relationship. But the question that went viral hides a better one: when someone is jealous of a video game, who exactly are they jealous of?

A girlfriend who expects a reply

According to Gulf News, Haas's objection is that the game asks you to maintain a relationship with a woman: text her, take her out, and she reacts badly if you ignore her. Notice that, in this version, the list says nothing about sex. It is about presence, care, answering back. That is a pretty accurate description of what any relationship asks of you.

And the romance, the same report notes, citing IGN, is optional. Jason and Lucia start out wanting one, but the player decides whether to pursue it; skip it and the two are less devoted, though still loyal when it counts. The game doesn't force anyone to date Lucia. What it offers is somewhere for attention to go.

Freud's three layers of jealousy

In 1922, Freud published a short paper, Some Neurotic Mechanisms in Jealousy, Paranoia and Homosexuality. He opens by saying that jealousy is one of those affective states, like grief, that may be described as normal. Then he adds, with a hint of mischief, that if anyone appears to be without it, we may infer that it has undergone severe repression and plays all the greater part in the unconscious. Anyone swearing they could never be jealous of a video game might want to reread that line.

Freud describes three layers: competitive or normal jealousy, projected jealousy and delusional jealousy. The last two don't belong in a social media argument, so they stay out of this one. I'll stick with the first, which he breaks down into four ingredients: grief, "the pain caused by the thought of losing the loved object"; the narcissistic wound; "feelings of enmity against the successful rival"; and a greater or lesser amount of self-criticism that tries to hold the person accountable for the loss.

Even this normal jealousy, Freud warns, is by no means completely rational, that is, proportionate to the real circumstances and under the full control of the conscious ego. It is rooted deep in the unconscious, carries on the earliest stirrings of the child's emotional life and originates in the Oedipus complex. Put loosely: every rival today is heir to an older one, the first who stole the attention of someone we loved.

Who is the successful rival?

Run the four ingredients through the TikTok question and something jumps out. Lucia is an odd rival: she doesn't exist and can't take anyone away. As a target for hostility, though, she works very well, because she has a face, a name and a romance the player can cultivate. My reading is that she gives shape to a rival with no face at all: time and attention.

What one fears losing here is not anyone's fidelity. It is the evenings on the couch, the conversation over dinner, the whole person in the room. The narcissistic wound changes shape too: imagining an affair with a character hurts less than losing to a controller. Self-criticism does the rest, with the question nobody posts: what am I missing, if a game can win? None of this requires believing that pixels are a lover. It only requires the fear of becoming background.

Leonida's couple, inside out

Earlier in this series, I wrote about Jason and Lucia and the curious place the game gives the player: a third party living inside the fictional couple, looking after the relationship and pressing the button for the next heist. The TikTok debate draws the same triangle inside out. Now the couple is flesh and blood, and the third party is the game.

Except this third party isn't competing for anyone's love. It is competing for hours. And hours, unlike Lucia, are real.

Fantasy is not an act, but the clock is real

In Creative Writers and Day-Dreaming (1908), Freud notes that children take play very seriously, invest it with large amounts of emotion, and still distinguish it quite well from reality. The opposite of play, he writes, is not what is serious but what is real. Adults, on the other hand, are ashamed of their fantasies, hide them as their most intimate possessions and, as a rule, would rather confess their misdeeds than share what they fantasize about.

By that standard, calling what happens in Leonida cheating treats play as an act, and the "it's just a game" defense is right about Lucia. But only half right. The fantasy stays on the screen; the time spent on it comes out of the same clock a couple shares. "It's just a game" is right about the content and wrong about the calendar. Maybe that is why arguments about fiction don't settle this: it was never about fiction.

In the end, the TikTok question applies to far more than GTA 6. Every couple has faceless rivals: work that spills into the evening, the series that never ends, the phone on the dinner table. In your home, which one wins most often? And when jealousy hits, is it of the person or of the hours?
